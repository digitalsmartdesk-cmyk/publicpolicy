Household debt reached 45.9% of GDP in March 2026

Since mid-2022, household borrowing has grown almost twice as fast as the economy and faster than household financial savings. In 2025-26 new borrowing rose to 6.2% of GDP while net financial savings fell to 6.3%. This draft covers RBI data to March 2026 and BIS data to December 2025.

Findings

Statements of what the data show. Judgment follows further down, kept separate.

  1. Households owed ₹158.5 lakh crore at the end of March 2026, which is 45.9% of GDP. In June 2022 the figure was 36.4%.
  2. The ratio rose in 14 of the last 15 quarters. It was flat in one (June 2024) and did not fall in any.
  3. Over the same period debt grew 78%, household financial assets 49% and nominal GDP 41%.
  4. Households added ₹21.5 lakh crore of debt in 2025-26, which raised the debt stock by 15.7%. New borrowing was 6.2% of GDP, up from 4.9% in 2024-25.
  5. Net financial savings were 6.3% of GDP in 2025-26, down from 7.1% in 2024-25 and up from 5.3% in 2022-23. The fall is a one-year move.
  6. New borrowing equalled about half (49.8%) of the gross financial assets households added in 2025-26. In 2024-25 it was about 41%.
  7. Debt per ₹100 of financial assets rose from ₹27.1 to ₹32.3. That is the highest of the 16 quarters in the RBI series.
  8. Financial assets fell from 152.2% of GDP in September 2024 to 141.9% in March 2026. Equity and mutual funds make up 28.6% of them and move with market prices.
  9. Banks hold 81.2% of household debt and other financial institutions 18.8%.
  10. On the BIS measure, credit to households was 47.8% of GDP in the fourth quarter of 2025. That is the highest in a series that begins in 2007, when it peaked at 46.2%. Among seven countries, India ranks fifth, between China (58.0%) and Brazil (37.6%).

How to read this

Judgment, not measurement.

  • The debt-to-assets ratio is a rough guide. Financial assets leave out gold and property, which many households hold, so ₹32 of debt per ₹100 of financial assets overstates debt against total household wealth.
  • The series covers all households together. It cannot say whether the households that borrow are the ones that hold the deposits and shares. That needs household-level data, which is the aim of the January brief.
  • "Savings down, debt up" describes 2025-26 against 2024-25. Over the four years from 2022-23, net financial savings rose from 5.3% to 6.3% of GDP, so the savings side has not fallen over the longer period. The debt side has risen steadily.
  • The RBI is reported to say the rise is driven mainly by non-housing retail loans. That is a broad group that includes personal, vehicle, credit card and gold loans, so it does not show that households are borrowing to consume.
  • Because equity and mutual funds are 28.6% of financial assets, part of the swings in the asset ratio reflects prices. This draft does not separate price changes from new saving.
  • India's household credit ratio is below that of China, the United States, Thailand and South Korea. A lower ratio does not by itself mean lower risk, because the pace of increase and the share of unsecured loans also matter.

Four different household-debt figures are in circulation. The headline here uses the first.

  • The updated research document now separates the September 2025 figure (45.5%) from March 2026 (45.8%). It still says to quote the lower figure as the conservative one, which compares two different dates. This page quotes the latest Bulletin figure, 45.9%, and labels the measure.
  • The document's asset ratios of 152.6% (September 2024) and 141.6% (March 2026) come from the August edition. The September edition revises them to 152.2% and 141.9%. This draft uses the September edition throughout and records the vintage on every row of the data sheet.
  • The document gives the 2007 BIS level as 45.4%. That is the first quarter of the series (Q2 2007). The 2007 high is 46.2% in Q3 2007. The 2016 low of 33.1%, the 2020 high of 44.3% and the latest 47.8% match the document.
  • Chapter plan alignment. (a) The chapter 1 plan leads with the BIS 47.8%, and one working title is "47.8%", while the plan also says to headline the RBI series. This page headlines the RBI figure. (b) The chapter 3 working title "Savings Down, Debt Up" fits only the last year, as set out under "How to read this". (c) The chapter 4 plan describes the 58.4% as a share of "retail credit". RMA India, Business Today and The India Forum all describe it as a share of household borrowing, so the plan's wording should change. The date of the 58.4% is still unconfirmed. (d) The AI indicator launches in December in the roadmap but in months 4 to 8 in the chapter plan.
  • The annual flow table uses the new GDP base year (2022-23), while the stock ratios use the NSO's 2025-26 estimates of 5 June 2026. The two sets of ratios are not directly comparable, and older editions of the flow table use the old base, so I did not extend the savings series back before 2022-23. As a cross-check, 2025-26 new borrowing of ₹21.51 lakh crore matches the 12-month rise in the debt stock (₹21.51 lakh crore).
  • The document says the sources do not show what is driving the rise. The Financial Stability Report, as reported, points to non-housing retail loans. Chart 10 uses that, but it is a press report to be checked.
  • Source check, 18 October 2026. I found the Financial Stability Report of 30 June 2026 on the RBI site, but its PDFs show a human-verification page to automated readers, so I could not read the text. I did not work around this. Instead I compared independent press reports of the report. The 58.4% share, the 42.4% gold-loan growth rate and the 6.4% fintech delinquency each appear in two or three outlets with matching wording. That makes them likely but not verified. Someone needs to open Chapter I of the report and check them.
  • The 6.4% delinquency figure applies to fintech lenders' personal loans below ₹50,000, not to all small-ticket loans. Business Today and The India Forum describe it more loosely and add "up from about 4.5% two years earlier". The Economic Times report of the Financial Stability Report gives no earlier figure, so I removed the comparison.
  • Trading Economics shows 49.1% for the first quarter of 2026 on the BIS measure. I could not confirm it in the FRED series, which was last updated before that quarter, so it is not used.
